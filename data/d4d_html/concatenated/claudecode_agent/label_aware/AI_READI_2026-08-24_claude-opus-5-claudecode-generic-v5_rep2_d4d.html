Motivation

For what purpose was the dataset created?

NameResponse
Enable AI and machine learning research into type 2 diabetes salutogenesis
To enable future generations of artificial intelligence and machine learning research to provide critical insights into type 2 diabetes mellitus, including salutogenic pathways back to health. T2DM is a growing public health threat, yet current understanding of it — especially in the context of salutogenesis — is limited; AI-based approaches may improve that understanding, but a key obstacle is the lack of data ready for training AI models, which this dataset is intended to supply.
Serve as a model for future AI-ready dataset creation
To establish standards, best practices and guidelines for the collection, preparation and sharing of data for AI and machine learning purposes, so that the approach can be reused by other efforts building health datasets targeted at AI and machine learning. The project frames this as a blueprint for future data generation projects, covering project management as well as data collection, management and sharing.

Flagship Dataset of Type 2 Diabetes from the AI-READI Project
Flagship Dataset of Type 2 Diabetes from the AI-READI Project
Release 3.0.0 of the AI-READI dataset, a multimodal cross-sectional dataset collected from 2280 participants with and without type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) between 19 July 2023 and 1 May 2025 and harmonized across three data collection sites (the University of Alabama at Birmingham, the University of California San Diego, and the University of Washington). AI-READI is one of the data generation projects of the National Institutes of Health Common Fund Bridge2AI program. Its stated goal is to enable artificial intelligence and machine learning research into the pathogenesis and salutogenesis of T2DM — salutogenesis being the pathway from disease back to health. Recruitment sampling aimed at an approximately equal distribution of participants across self-reported race and ethnicity, biological sex, and four diabetes severity groups, and the acquisition protocol spans survey data, physical measurements, clinical laboratory data, retinal imaging, electrocardiography, continuous glucose monitoring, wearable activity monitoring and home environmental sensing. The release comprises 356,343 files totaling approximately 3.82 TB, organized under the Clinical Dataset Structure (CDS) v0.1.1 into one directory per data type, then modality, then device, then participant. The released data contain no protected health information; information on the sex and race or ethnicity of participants and on medications used has been removed from the public release and is held under controlled access.

Absence of AI-ready multimodal datasets for diabetes research
The ability to understand and affect the course of complex, multi-organ diseases such as T2DM has been limited by a lack of well-designed, high-quality, large and inclusive multimodal datasets. A major barrier to applying AI to the problem has been the absence of diverse datasets that are AI ready for training AI models, and the dataset is intended to fill that gap.
Under-representation of racial and ethnic groups in past T2DM research
Certain populations experience a greater burden of type 2 diabetes, which exacerbates health disparities, and past research studies have under-represented the groups bearing that higher burden. A meta-epidemiological review of T2DM studies published between 2000 and 2020 found that samples lacked racial and ethnic diversity. The cohort is therefore recruited to be approximately balanced across four self-reported race and ethnicity groups rather than to match the demographic distribution of the United States population.

    Acquisition Details
    Acquisition varied by domain. Some data were directly observable — laboratory results, vital signs and retinal imaging — while other data were reported by participants, such as survey responses. Verification of data entry was performed where possible, for example by cross-referencing entered medications against medications physically brought in or photographed by the participant. Mechanism DetailsName
REDCap was used to collect participant-reported questionnaire data and the following clinical data from the visit: medications assessment, vital signs, visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, monofilament test results and complete blood count results. Participants were directed into the REDCap interface by personalized links, access codes and QR codes in mailed and emailed invitations, where they could read a study overview, download the informed consent document, complete a screening survey and enroll by signing the electronic consent. Clinical values outside expected minimum and maximum ranges were flagged in REDCap for review.
REDCap electronic data capture
Both eyes were imaged on each participant using devices chosen for their different but partially overlapping modalities, with all retinal images collected under dilation except those from the Optomed. The devices and their scans were: the Optomed Aurora IQ handheld fundus camera (macula- and disc-centered undilated color fundus photography, 4 images per participant, DICOM); the iCare EIDON widefield truecolor confocal fundus system (12 images per participant, DICOM); the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA OCT and OCTA system (optic nerve head radial circle high resolution OCT, posterior pole macula 61-line OCT, and 20x20 512-line macular OCTA; 6 images per participant, DICOM); the Topcon Maestro2 3D OCT-1 (3D wide 12x9, 3D macula 6x6, macular OCTA 6x6; 6 images per participant, exported as .fda and converted to DICOM); the Topcon Triton DRI OCT (3D horizontal plus radial 12x9, macular OCTA 6x6 and 12x12; 6 images per participant, exported as .fda and converted to DICOM); the Zeiss Cirrus 5000 (disc cube, macula cube, macular and disc OCTA; 8 images per participant, DICOM); and the Heidelberg fluorescence lifetime imaging ophthalmoscope (macula-centered; 2 images per participant, exported as .sdt and converted to DICOM).
Retinal imaging devices
A Dexcom G6 real-time integrated continuous glucose monitoring system was applied to the participant's abdomen and worn continuously for 10 days after the visit, recording blood glucose in mg/dL every five minutes and exporting to CSV. Participants were masked to the readings during the monitoring period and received a Dexcom report by encrypted email afterwards giving hourly, daily and overall average glucose and a Glucose Management Indicator. Participants already wearing a Dexcom G6 could either place an additional study sensor at least three inches from their own or share their existing device's data through Dexcom Clarity, and could optionally allow download of up to 90 days of previously collected values, for up to 100 days of glucose data in total.
Dexcom G6 continuous glucose monitor
A Garmin Vivosmart 5 fitness tracker was worn for 10 days concurrently with the continuous glucose monitor and the home environmental sensor, recording number of steps, heart rate, sleep duration for circadian and diurnal rhythm, and oxygen saturation. Participants were advised to wear it on the non-dominant wrist but were free to choose, and returned it with a sheet recording which wrist was used and which hand was dominant. Data were exported in .FIT format and converted to the Open mHealth standard for the dataset. The device was chosen with participant privacy as a primary concern: it captures no video or audio, has no GPS location monitoring, and is not synced with participant-owned devices.
Garmin Vivosmart 5 physical activity monitor
A custom-designed environmental sensor unit, the LeeLab Anura, built at the Karalis Johnson Retina Center of the University of Washington, was placed by the participant in a frequently used area of the home for 10 days and returned by mail with the other devices, the participant noting where it had been placed. It recorded ambient temperature, relative humidity, nitrogen oxides (NO and NO2), volatile organic compounds, particulate matter (PM1.0, PM2.5, PM4 and PM10) and 11 multi-spectral light intensity measurements, exported to CSV. Like the fitness tracker it captures no video or audio, has no GPS, and is not synced with participant-owned devices.
LeeLab Anura home environmental sensor
A 12-lead ECG was performed on a Philips PageWriter TC30 Cardiograph while the participant sat in a reclining chair or lay supine, with the chair position recorded relative to supine as 0, 30, 60 or 90 degrees. Data were exported in .xml format to local storage and are distributed in WFDB format.
Twelve-lead electrocardiography
A general cognitive screen was carried out using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, administered electronically on an iPad using the MoCA Duo application, which captures participant responses interactively. The instrument evaluates memory, executive functioning, attention, language, visuospatial and visuoconstructional skills, orientation, conceptual thinking and calculations, producing a total score and six domain-specific index scores. Total score, section subscores, Memory Index Score and task completion times were exported in .csv format.
Cognitive screening with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment
Visual acuity and contrast sensitivity were assessed under both photopic and mesopic conditions, with right and left eyes tested separately. Photopic letter visual acuity was measured with the M&S Technologies Electronic Visual Acuity tester at 4 meters from a 12-by-20-inch touch-screen monitor under best-corrected conditions and expressed as the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution; mesopic acuity was measured through a 2.0 neutral density filter. Photopic letter contrast sensitivity was measured monocularly with the MARS chart from Mars Perceptrix under background luminance of 60 to 120 cd/m2 (optimally 85), illuminance of 189 to 377 lux (optimally 267) and a viewing distance of 40 to 59 cm (designed 50 cm), with near correction worn and testing terminated after two consecutive errors or the end of the chart; mesopic contrast sensitivity was assessed through the neutral density filter. Autorefraction was obtained for each eye with a Topcon KR 800 autorefractor. Lensometers used across sites were the NIDEK LM-600P and the Topcon CL-200 and CL-300.
Visual function testing
Peripheral neuropathy was assessed with the monofilament test, applying a standard 10 g filament to three locations on the plantar surface of each foot, ten times per location, with shoes and socks removed and the participant's eyes closed while responding yes or no to each application. Insensate areas were retested for confirmation, and the sequence was repeated randomly at each testing site on each foot until results were obtained. Results were recorded on an iPad, laptop or paper questionnaire and entered into REDCap by clinical research staff.
Peripheral neuropathy monofilament testing
Height, weight, and waist and hip circumference were measured, followed by calculation of the waist-hip ratio and body mass index. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate were measured twice, two minutes apart, with an automatic oscillometric medically approved device. Blood pressure devices used across the sites were the OMRON HEM 907XL, the Medline MDS4001 automatic digital monitor, and the Welch Allyn 6000 series vital signs monitor with a Welch Allyn FlexiPort reusable cuff.
Vital signs and physical measurements
Approximately 50 to 60 mL of non-fasting blood and a urine specimen were collected during the visit. Complete blood count testing was performed on fresh whole blood by laboratories certified under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments local to each site; all other laboratory tests were performed on stored frozen samples by the University of Washington Nutrition and Obesity Research Center testing facility, with results supplied in .csv format. Samples analyzed at that facility were first stored locally at the University of Alabama at Birmingham and the University of California San Diego at -70 degrees Celsius, then batch-shipped on dry ice approximately once per quarter.
Clinical laboratory testing of blood and urine
Blood derivatives are processed into a central study-wide biobank at the Center for Clinical and Translational Science of the University of Alabama at Birmingham. Local processing of plasma, serum and buffy coats follows standardized operating procedures at each site on the day of collection. Plasma, serum and buffy coats collected at the University of Washington and the University of California San Diego are batch-shipped to Birmingham; genomic DNA extraction from stored buffy coats is performed there, as is centralized processing of CPT tubes for peripheral blood mononuclear cells, with tubes pre-centrifuged locally and delivered overnight. PAXgene RNA vacutainers are stable for up to 72 hours after collection, allowing overnight shipment. Derivatives are separated into small aliquots and stored at -80 degrees Celsius or, for peripheral blood mononuclear cells, at cryogenic temperatures.

Several quality control measures were used at the time of data entry and acquisition. Clinical data outside expected minimum and maximum ranges were flagged in REDCap and appeared in reports viewed by clinical research coordinators and data managers.
